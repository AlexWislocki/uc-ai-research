How do Computer Science majors at UC talk about AI?

An exploration of how undergraduate computer science students communicate their views on generative AI in programming assignments—and what those conversations reveal about learning and academic integrity.

Alexander T. Wislocki · ENG 2089: Intermediate Composition · November 18, 2026
01 · The community

A conversation happening inside computer science

This project focuses on undergraduate computer science majors at the University of Cincinnati who participate in conversations about coursework, projects, tools, careers, and the future of the field.

Where the community connects

These conversations can happen in CS classes, before or after class, at student organization meet-ups, during study sessions, and through career-related programs. AI stands out because students are learning how it works, how to design it, and how to use it.

As AI has expanded beyond computer science, the community's conversations have evolved alongside the technology.

Central research question

How do undergraduate computer science majors communicate their views on using generative AI for programming assignments, and what do these discussions reveal about their community’s values regarding learning and academic integrity?

Listening to the community

The project combines an anonymous peer survey, a community event artifact, and secondary research.

Anonymous survey

The survey was designed to encourage honest answers without participants worrying that their responses would be tracked. Its sections covered:

  • Background and AI usage
  • Opinions and perspectives on AI
  • Academic integrity and learning
  • Communication within the discourse community
  • An optional open-ended question about what the community values

Additional evidence

The research also considered an email invitation to a UCAI Society event, “Applied AI: From Study Tools to Breakthrough Research,” distributed to the College of Engineering and Applied Sciences, along with a related published study by Alanezi and colleagues (2026).

Open-ended responses

Eight of the 14 participants chose to answer the optional final question about what the computer science community values most in programming and AI use. One response exceeded 350 words. The essay highlights concerns including learning, efficiency, data privacy, overreliance, and whether AI-generated work reflects a student's own understanding.

04 · Discussion

Not simply pro-AI or anti-AI

The survey suggests that students are negotiating how AI can be useful without undermining the purpose of learning to program.

Conversation is part of the community

In-person conversations with classmates were the most commonly reported setting. The essay also points to organized AI information sessions, including the UCAI Society event, as places where students can encounter new perspectives and continue discussion.

Disagreement is common

Most respondents had disagreed with another computer science student about whether AI use in programming assignments is acceptable. The optional written responses also suggest that participants have detailed opinions they want to express.

Comfort levels are divided

Although the largest group felt somewhat comfortable using AI-generated code, some respondents were very uncomfortable. The essay connects this uncertainty to concerns about academic dishonesty and compromising one's education.

Context matters for integrity

Eleven of 14 respondents said AI use is sometimes academic dishonesty, depending on how it is used. No respondent selected “never.” This points toward a community still working out boundaries rather than applying one simple rule.

The central tension

Students want AI to be a helpful tool, but not at the cost of learning, honest work, or the ability to understand and stand behind their own code. The discussion is ongoing because the community is trying to find that balance.

05 · Conclusion

Building an ethical foundation for the future

The conversations about AI matter not only for students’ current assignments, but also for their future careers and lifelong learning.

The essay argues that computer science students want a foundation of beliefs and ethics around AI use: protecting their own work and careers, respecting the work of others, and recognizing innovation without being careless with it.

The goal is not to reject innovation, but to use it in ways students can understand, defend, and feel proud of.
